Featured explainer

Why is Elon Musk talking about 10 gigawatts?

The short version: AI needs a lot of computing equipment, and that equipment needs enormous amounts of electricity, cooling, networking, and investment.

The story in 60 seconds

Imagine a school for a very smart student

Training an AI is like teaching a student using huge numbers of examples. Answering people's questions is like that student using what they learned. Both activities need computers.

Powerful chips do the calculations. Data centers house the servers. Cloud providers rent computing capacity to customers over the internet.

NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang said SpaceX is now being treated as a cloud-service-provider customer as well as a company using computing for its own AI. Musk has discussed aiming for around 10 gigawatts of computing capacity by the end of 2027.

Why it matters: AI growth depends not only on software, but also on chips, power, cooling, networking, and whether the investment pays off.

One important distinction
10 GW

Gigawatts measure power—not computing speed.

One gigawatt is one billion watts. In this headline, the number is used to describe the huge power/infrastructure scale being targeted. The actual number of AI calculations depends on the hardware and how it is configured.

At a continuous 10 GW draw, energy use would be 240 gigawatt-hours per day. That is a hypothetical illustration, not a claim that the target is already operating.

Build your foundation

Four concepts worth understanding

Start with the basics, then connect them to real systems and your career.

🧠
AI

Training vs. inference

Training: adjusting a model using examples. Inference: using the trained model to produce an answer or prediction.

Everyday analogy: studying for an exam versus answering a question during the exam.

☁️
Cloud

Cloud computing

Rent computing resources over a network instead of buying and maintaining every server yourself.

Everyday analogy: renting a car when needed instead of owning a fleet.

🏢
Infrastructure

Data centers

Buildings or facilities containing servers, storage, networking, power systems, and cooling.

Everyday analogy: a library plus a power station and a delivery network, all designed for computers.

🛠️
DevOps

Automation & reliability

DevOps combines practices and tools that help teams deliver software reliably, monitor it, and improve it.

Everyday analogy: a repeatable checklist and automated assembly line instead of doing every task manually.

🧩
Hardware

GPU vs. CPU

A CPU is a flexible general-purpose processor. A GPU can handle many similar calculations in parallel, which is useful for many AI workloads.

Neither is automatically best for every job.

📈
Business

Demand isn't profit

More customers and bigger infrastructure can create opportunity—but power bills, hardware costs, debt, competition, and utilization affect profitability.

Ask: who pays, what does it cost, and can the service scale sustainably?

Mini lesson: What is a cloud service provider (CSP)?

A cloud service provider offers computing services—such as servers, storage, databases, or AI infrastructure—over a network. Examples include AWS, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud. Customers pay according to the service and pricing model they choose.

Mini lesson: Why do AI data centers need so much electricity?

AI accelerators and other servers consume power while calculating. They also produce heat, so facilities need cooling, networking, backup systems, and reliable electricity delivery. Efficiency matters: useful work per unit of energy is an important measure.

Mini lesson: Where does DevOps fit?

Cloud infrastructure must be configured, updated, secured, monitored, and recovered when things fail. DevOps skills such as Linux, scripting, Git, CI/CD, containers, infrastructure as code, observability, and cloud fundamentals help teams operate systems consistently.
